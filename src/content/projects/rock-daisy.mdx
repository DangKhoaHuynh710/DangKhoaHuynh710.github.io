## Overview

Rock Daisy was the payload on Galah, the USYD Rocketry Team's 2026 rocket. It was a 3U CubeSat-sized experiment that tested one step towards mining the Moon: sorting ilmenite out of lunar soil with an electric field. Ilmenite is an iron-titanium mineral and a candidate source of oxygen on the Moon. Separating it is part of in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU), which means making what a mission needs from local material instead of launching it from Earth.

The experiment ran during the rocket's coast phase, in reduced gravity. A motor-driven agitator rubbed ilmenite simulant against polypropylene to charge it. A servo then released the particles between two plates 15 mm apart with 7 kV across them, a field of about 467 kV/m, and a camera filmed the stream as the field pushed it sideways. Everything sat inside a sealed aluminium chamber pumped down to 5 Pa, to reproduce the vacuum of space.

At the International Rocket Engineering Competition (IREC) 2026 in Texas, Rock Daisy won first place in the SDL Payload Challenge.

## My role

I was the payload's primary electronics engineer, responsible for everything electrical from schematic to flight. I:

- designed the flight computer's schematic and printed circuit board (PCB) through three versions, and assembled the flight board;
- wrote the flight firmware: five FreeRTOS tasks and the launch-triggered flight state machine;
- built the WiFi checkout dashboard used for bring-up and pre-flight testing;
- added a second ESP32-S3 as a camera node, recording in-flight video on an OV5640 camera;
- designed the switching for the 7 kV high-voltage module;
- designed and built the payload's internal cable harness and connector scheme;
- helped build the Python post-flight analysis that turns telemetry and video into a report.

This page focuses on the electronics. The vacuum chamber, internal frame and particle dispenser were designed by the wider payload team, and the vacuum feed-through board by Charlie Balderstone, Galah's technical director.

## Design decisions

### Two microcontrollers with separate jobs

The flight computer uses two ESP32-S3 microcontrollers. We chose the ESP32-S3 for its number of input and output pins and its built-in WiFi, which made a wireless checkout possible.

The first one runs the experiment. It reads a high-g accelerometer (ADXL375) and a barometer (MS5607) on a 20 MHz serial peripheral interface (SPI) bus, logs to a microSD card, keeps time with a real-time clock, and drives the motor, servo, buzzer and high-voltage supply. The second one only runs the camera. The two are linked by a 40 MHz SPI connection, which lets the checkout dashboard on the first board show live images from the second.

The first board uses both of its processor cores. One core decides the flight state. The other watches the telemetry stream and acts as a watchdog, so a single false sensor reading cannot push the payload into the wrong state.

Power comes from a three-cell lithium-ion battery. On-board regulators make 5 V and 3.3 V for the sensors, servo and controllers. The unregulated battery rail feeds a Pololu motor driver for the DC motor.

### Launch detection that ignores bumps

A payload that starts its experiment on the pad is a failed payload, so the launch detector has to respond to a motor burn and nothing else. The firmware samples the accelerometer at 800 Hz, filters it, and looks at the last second of readings. It declares launch only when at least half of those samples exceed 35 m/s², about 3.5 g. A knock during handling is too short to count. A rocket motor is not.

At launch, the agitator motor starts charging the particles and the camera node starts recording. Four seconds later (T+4 s), the servo releases the particles and the high-voltage supply and buzzer switch on. At T+29 s every output returns to its safe, inactive state. All T+ times count from the launch declaration, not from physical lift-off.

At power-up, a mode-select pin chooses between test mode for the bench and flight mode. The board then starts the SPI bus, microSD card, non-volatile storage, sensors, wireless link and actuator outputs in turn. Each one reports whether it started, and any failure is saved to non-volatile storage and reported over USB and WiFi.

### Keeping 7 kV off until it should be on

The plates are driven by an XP Power E70 high-voltage module. Its output scales with its input, reaching 7 kV only at 15 V, so the battery supply is boosted to 15 V at its input to get the full 7 kV. The module is current-limited. A supply like that must never switch on while people are handling the payload, so the high voltage sits behind two hardware layers.

- **Two switches in series.** A relay and an N-channel MOSFET sit in series in the supply path. Each is driven by its own signal line, and both close only after launch has been detected. One stuck output or firmware fault cannot energise the plates on its own.
- **A limit switch as an interlock.** The high voltage can only activate when the module is enclosed and cannot be touched.

### Working inside a sealed aluminium box

The vacuum chamber made three electrical problems that a normal payload does not have.

**Getting connections through the wall.** Every external connection, including the status lights and buzzer, the remove-before-flight pin and the radio link, has to pass through the chamber wall without leaking. We compared commercial vacuum feed-throughs, hermetic military-spec connectors and a custom PCB feed-through. The commercial options cost far more, and their flanged mounts would have taken internal volume that a 3U payload cannot spare. We chose a custom PCB clamped between a retainer and the chamber body. An O-ring seals against a copper ring on the PCB, and sealed vias carry the signals through.

**Radio.** An aluminium box blocks radio, so with the chamber closed on the ground the WiFi antennas inside could not reach the outside world directly. The feed-through is the only path the signal has out of the chamber.

**Heat.** In a vacuum there is no air to carry heat away. A copper brace conducts heat from the internal electronics to the aluminium chamber, and the high-voltage board is heat-sunk into the chamber body through thermal pads.

## Testing and what broke

**A reset switch that fired in flight.** On an earlier version of the board, the reset switch was mechanically unstable, and it reset the board by accident during flight.

**No way to tell it was alive.** In versions 1 and 2, once the board was installed inside the payload there was no way to confirm it had powered on. Version 3 fixed this with test mode and a WiFi dashboard. On the bench it shows live telemetry and board health, fires the motor, servo, buzzer and high-voltage output one at a time, and pulls logs off the microSD card. In test mode, log timestamps are taken from the connected device's clock when the dashboard connects, rather than from the real-time clock.

Before flight the payload passed four formal tests:

- vacuum chamber test, 15 January 2026;
- agitator and actuators test, 28 March 2026;
- bench test, 8 April 2026;
- plastics structural integrity test, 8 April 2026.

The video analysis was checked the same way. The same pipeline was run on a stationary bench recording and on a vibration-only control, using the same camera, particle feed and high-voltage hardware. That separates what the electric field does from what rocket vibration does.

## Result

Rock Daisy won first place in the SDL Payload Challenge at IREC 2026, held at Spaceport Midland in Texas. The competition drew 150 teams from more than 20 countries, and the USYD Rocketry Team became the first team to win the SDL Payload Challenge twice.

In flight, the camera recorded the particle stream through the high-voltage window. The analysis pipeline then tracked the stream frame by frame. It finds the two plate edges, splits the gap into horizontal bands, takes the centre of the dark particle stream in each band, and fits a line through those centres. The stream's position is measured from 0 at the negative plate to 1 at the positive plate.

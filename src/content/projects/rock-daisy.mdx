## Overview

Rock Daisy was the payload on Galah, the USYD Rocketry Team's 2026 rocket. It won first place in the SDL Payload Challenge at the International Rocket Engineering Competition (IREC) 2026 in Texas.

The experiment tested electrostatic separation of ilmenite, an iron-titanium mineral in lunar soil and a candidate source of oxygen on the Moon. Sorting it out of the soil is a step towards in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU): producing what a mission needs from local material instead of launching it from Earth.

During the rocket's coast phase, a motor-driven agitator charged ilmenite simulant by friction against polypropylene. A servo then released it between two plates 15 mm apart held at 7 kV, a field of about 467 kV/m, while a camera recorded the particle stream. The experiment ran inside a sealed aluminium chamber pumped down to 5 Pa.

## My role

I was the primary electronics engineer, responsible for the payload's electronics from schematic to flight:

- **Flight computer:** schematic, PCB layout and assembly, over three board revisions
- **Firmware:** five FreeRTOS tasks and the launch-triggered flight state machine
- **Camera node:** a second ESP32-S3 recording in-flight video on an OV5640 camera
- **Checkout dashboard:** WiFi interface used for bring-up and pre-flight testing
- **High voltage:** switching and interlocks for the 7 kV supply
- **Wiring:** the payload's internal cable harness and connector scheme
- **Analysis:** with the team, the Python pipeline that turns telemetry and video into a post-flight report

Other members of the payload team designed the vacuum chamber, internal frame and particle dispenser. Charlie Balderstone, Galah's technical director, designed the vacuum feed-through board.

## Design decisions

### Flight computer architecture

The flight computer splits the work between two ESP32-S3 microcontrollers, chosen for their number of input and output pins and built-in WiFi.

| | Flight computer, revision 3 |
|---|---|
| Experiment controller | ESP32-S3: sensors, logging, flight state machine, actuators |
| Camera controller | ESP32-S3 with an OV5640 camera |
| Link between them | 40 MHz SPI, so the checkout dashboard can show live images |
| Sensors | ADXL375 high-g accelerometer and MS5607 barometer on a 20 MHz SPI bus |
| Storage and time | microSD card and a battery-backed real-time clock |
| Actuators | DC motor (Pololu driver), servo, buzzer, 7 kV high-voltage module |
| Power | Three-cell lithium-ion battery, 5 V and 3.3 V regulators, and a 15 V boosted supply for the high-voltage module |

The experiment controller uses both of its cores. One runs the flight state machine. The other monitors the telemetry stream as a watchdog, which protects the flight logic from a single false sensor reading.

### Launch detection and flight sequence

The firmware samples the accelerometer at 800 Hz, low-pass filters it, and evaluates a one-second sliding window. Launch is declared only when at least half of the samples in that window exceed 35 m/s², about 3.5 g. Short knocks during handling do not trigger it.

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| T+0 s | Launch declared. The agitator motor starts charging the particles and the camera starts recording. |
| T+4 s | The servo releases the particles, and the high-voltage supply and buzzer switch on. |
| T+29 s | Every output returns to its safe, inactive state. |

T+ times count from the software launch declaration, not physical lift-off. At power-up, a mode-select pin chooses between bench test mode and flight mode. The board then starts each subsystem in turn: SPI bus, microSD card, non-volatile storage, sensors, wireless link and actuator outputs. Any failure is saved to non-volatile storage and reported over USB and WiFi.

### High-voltage safety

The plates are driven by an XP Power E70 module, which is current-limited and gives its full 7 kV only at a 15 V input, so its supply is boosted from the battery to 15 V. Two hardware layers keep it off until it is needed:

- **Two switches in series:** a relay and an N-channel MOSFET, each driven by its own signal line, both closed only after launch detection. A single stuck output or firmware fault cannot energise the plates.
- **Limit-switch interlock:** the high voltage can only activate when the module is enclosed.

### Electronics inside a sealed vacuum chamber

The aluminium vacuum chamber created three electrical problems:

- **Connections through the wall:** every external connection, including the status lights, buzzer, remove-before-flight pin and radio link, has to pass through the chamber wall without leaking. We compared commercial vacuum feed-throughs, hermetic military-spec connectors and a custom PCB. The commercial options cost far more, and their flanged mounts took internal volume a 3U payload cannot spare. The custom PCB is clamped between a retainer and the chamber body, with an O-ring sealing against a copper ring on the board and sealed vias carrying the signals.
- **Radio:** the aluminium chamber blocks radio, so with it closed on the ground, the feed-through is the only path for the WiFi signal to get out.
- **Heat:** with no air in the chamber, nothing carries heat away by convection. A copper brace conducts heat from the internal electronics to the chamber wall, and the high-voltage board is heat-sunk to the chamber through thermal pads.

## Testing and what broke

- **Unstable reset switch:** on an earlier board revision, the reset switch was mechanically unstable and reset the board during flight.
- **No power-on confirmation:** in revisions 1 and 2, there was no way to confirm the board had powered on once it was installed in the payload. Revision 3 added a test mode and the WiFi dashboard, which shows live telemetry and board health, fires the motor, servo, buzzer and high-voltage output one at a time, and downloads logs from the microSD card.

The video analysis was also checked against controls. The same pipeline ran on a stationary bench recording and on a vibration-only recording, using the same camera, particle feed and high-voltage hardware, to separate the effect of the electric field from the effect of rocket vibration.

## Result

Rock Daisy won first place in the SDL Payload Challenge at IREC 2026, held at Spaceport Midland in Texas, against 150 teams from more than 20 countries. The USYD Rocketry Team is the first team to win the SDL Payload Challenge twice.

In flight, the camera recorded the particle stream through the high-voltage window. The analysis pipeline tracks the stream frame by frame: it finds both plate edges, splits the gap into horizontal bands, locates the centre of the dark particle stream in each band, and fits a line through those centres. Stream position is measured from 0 at the negative plate to 1 at the positive plate.

## Overview

Project Kookaburra is the USYD Rocketry Team's next rocket. Before it can launch, equipment at the pad has to fill it with liquid nitrous oxide, operate the ground valves, disconnect the fill line and stay connected to the rocket's flight computers, all controlled from a flight line several hundred metres away. This is the ground support equipment (GSE), and its electronics live in the pad box, a weatherproof enclosure at the launch pad.

On Galah, the team's 2026 rocket, the pad box was built around an off-the-shelf data acquisition unit, a LabJack T7, with screw terminals and solid-state relay modules. As GSE co-lead I am replacing it with team-designed boards on a single RS-485 bus. I designed two of them: the Valve Control Board and the Umbilical and Quick Disconnect (QD) Board.

**Status (November 2026):** both boards have complete schematics and 4-layer printed circuit board (PCB) layouts and went through the team's Critical Design Review. They have not been built or tested, so every number on this page is a design value from manufacturers' datasheets.

## My role

As GSE co-lead I lead the electrical, electronics and software work for the team's ground systems, and coordinate their requirements, design reviews, testing and documentation. On the pad box:

- **Architecture:** defined the new system and the design rules every board follows
- **Board design:** the Valve Control Board and the Umbilical and QD Board, from schematic to 4-layer layout in Altium Designer
- **Briefs for new engineers:** wrote the design briefs they used for the Power Distribution Board, Command Hub and Sensor Nodes
- **Team resources:** wrote the team's PCB Schematic Design Guide (Rev 0.1, September 2026) and a new-member induction task, a single-channel valve switching board covering MOSFET versus relay selection, flyback and overcurrent protection, current sensing and connector choice
- **Design review:** led the electronics section of the team's Critical Design Review

## Design decisions

### System architecture

Each function now has its own small board, and every board follows the same rules:

- **One field bus:** every board talks RS-485, a robust long-distance wired standard, using Modbus RTU, a simple industrial protocol, over its own link from the Command Hub. Any new board that speaks RS-485 plugs straight into the system, so new features no longer depend on finding an off-the-shelf board that fits.
- **One trunk cable:** a single RJ45 cable carries both the RS-485 link and 12 V power to each remote board.
- **Digitise at the sensor:** Sensor Nodes sit next to the pressure, load-cell and temperature sensors and send digital readings, so long cable runs no longer add noise to analogue signals.
- **Connectors:** every connector latches and is keyed, with a different connector family for each voltage, so cables cannot work loose or be plugged into the wrong socket.
- **Migration:** the LabJack stays connected as a fallback while the new boards are brought up.

### Safety

The pad box controls pressurised oxidiser and the igniter, so hardware sets every safe default and firmware can only change state after boot. The system is designed to competition safety rules.

- **Emergency stop and arming key:** both are in series on the rail that powers the valve coils. Opening either one removes power from every valve, independent of the firmware.
- **Outputs fail off:** if a channel loses its control signal or gate-drive supply, the MOSFET gate is pulled low and the output switches off. Gates are also held off at power-up.
- **Igniter isolation:** the igniter channel is isolated from the rest of the board.
- **Power rails:** each Power Distribution Board rail needs both a physical latch button and a firmware command to switch on.

### Valve Control Board

The Valve Control Board switches power to the ground fill-system valves, the umbilical release and the igniter, and measures what each channel actually draws.

| | Valve Control Board |
|---|---|
| Channels | 12: nine at 24 V and three at 12 V |
| Switching | Low-side N-channel MOSFET with optocoupler gate driver and PWM |
| Channel current | Designed for 7 A |
| Control | Pad box mini-PC over RS-485, or the LabJack T7, selected by a jumper |
| Protection | Time-lag ceramic fuses (10 A and 6.3 A) and transient-voltage-suppression (TVS) diodes on the 24 V and 12 V rails; resettable fuse and TVS diode on the 5 V rail; flyback protection on the valve outputs |
| Sensing | TI INA237 with a 12 mΩ shunt on every channel: current and supply voltage, about 13.7 A range in 0.42 mA steps |
| Diagnostics | LED per channel, RGB status LED, fault buzzer, test points, microSD logging, battery-backed clock and error log |
| Microcontroller | STM32H755, dual-core Arm Cortex-M7 and Cortex-M4 |

- **MOSFETs instead of relays:** the new solenoid valves need pulse-width modulation (PWM) to set their position, which the old on/off relay modules could not provide. The Toshiba TK110E10PL MOSFET handles the vent valve's 6.1 A with margin; with a maximum on-resistance of 10.7 mΩ, its calculated conduction loss is about 0.52 W at 7 A.
- **Optocoupler gate drive:** the MOSFET needs a 10 V gate to switch fully on, which a 3.3 V microcontroller pin cannot provide. A Toshiba TLP152 drives the gate to about 12 V with around 1 A peak current for clean PWM edges, and isolates the logic from the 24 V side if a MOSFET fails.
- **Timing budget:** I calculated the delay through each stage from datasheet worst-case values and drew it as a datasheet-style timing diagram. Command to valve power takes under 0.3 µs, well below the valve's millisecond mechanical response.
- **Measured feedback:** the old system only showed what had been commanded. A 12 mΩ high-side shunt and a TI INA237 on each channel measure the current each valve actually draws, over a range of about 13.7 A in 0.42 mA steps. Worst-case datasheet accuracy is about ±25 mA at 7 A, enough to distinguish a working valve from an open or shorted coil. An RC filter at about 80 kHz on the sensor input removes MOSFET switching spikes with negligible added error.
- **Thermal design:** the launch site reaches 45 to 50 °C ambient, so the MOSFETs are mounted on shared heatsinks.
- **Fallback control:** a 2:1 multiplexer hands control back to the LabJack without rewiring, adding under 10 ns of delay.

### Umbilical and QD Board

The Umbilical and QD Board is the pad box's electrical link to the rocket until separation. It sits in its own enclosure.

| | Umbilical and QD Board |
|---|---|
| Vehicle link | USB host to the rocket's two flight computers, forward and aft |
| Pad box link | One RS-485 channel to the Command Hub |
| Vehicle valves | Carries main oxidiser, vent and dump valve power from the Valve Control Board and monitors each line's voltage |
| Quick disconnect | Extends and retracts the fill-line disconnect actuator, commanded from the flight line or from switches on the enclosure |
| Charging | 5 V to the forward and aft avionics; 24 V to the payload's three-cell lithium-ion pack, switched by a relay |
| Input protection | Resettable fuse, ideal diode and TVS diode on each input |

- **Two USB hosts, one microcontroller:** both flight computers are USB devices, so the board must host them simultaneously. The STM32H755 has two USB controllers, so a single chip serves both.
- **Ideal diode protection:** a TI LM73100 on each input survives reverse polarity down to −15 V and drops only about 28 mΩ times the load current, far less than a Schottky diode. I set its voltage window with resistor dividers: the 12 V input operates only between about 7.4 and 15.5 V, and the 5 V input between 3.6 and 6.5 V. If the wrong supply is connected, for example 12 V into the 5 V input, the output stays off and the chip tolerates up to 28 V. A soft-start capacitor limits the output rise to about 2 V/ms, keeping inrush current to tens of milliamps.
- **Actuator reversal:** a double-pole, double-throw relay with break-before-make contacts reverses the disconnect actuator without ever shorting the supply.
- **Payload charging:** the payload battery needs a permanent ground, but the Valve Control Board switches on the ground side. A relay on this board converts that output into a high-side switch, so the pad box can stop charging even though the payload board cannot communicate with it.
- **Pad-side control:** a push-button and toggle switch on the enclosure let the pad crew operate the disconnect during setup without a flight-line command.
- **Connector grouping:** power outputs use dual-row 4-pin connectors, grouped to match the terminal count of each magnetic connector on the rocket side.

## Testing and what broke

The boards have not been built yet. Each problem found while running the old pad box through a full launch campaign became a requirement for the new design:

| Problem with the old pad box | Response in the new design |
|---|---|
| The solid-state relay modules were limited to 3 A and on/off switching, but the new vent valve draws about 6.1 A at 24 V. | MOSFET channels designed for 7 A, with PWM on every channel. |
| Screw-terminal wires worked loose during operations, dropping sensor readings or valve control. | Latching, keyed connectors throughout, rated for the actual crimp terminal and wire gauge. |
| The control software showed what had been commanded, not what the hardware did. | Current and voltage measured on every valve channel. |
| On past trips, a pad box fault damaged a flight computer, and a flight computer fault damaged the pad box. | Fuses and TVS diodes on every valve-board rail, ideal diodes on the vehicle link and electronic fuses (eFuses) on the power distribution rails. |
| About AUD 1,100 per LabJack T7 and AUD 66.47 per relay module, with at least 10 channels needed. | Custom boards, with parts selected against datasheets and supplier availability. |
